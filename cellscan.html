CellScan

Can machine learning tell malignant from benign breast tumours, and could we trust it? A 10-day machine learning research project, with a live app you can try.

▶ Open the CellScan appRead the write-up

About This Project

Research question: can machine learning tell the difference between malignant (cancer) and benign (not cancer) breast tumours, and could we trust it if it did?

Tools: Python in Google Colab, using pandas (tables of data), matplotlib (graphs), scikit-learn (machine learning) and Gradio (building the app).

Data: the Wisconsin Diagnostic Breast Cancer dataset from the UCI Machine Learning Repository. It was collected at the University of Wisconsin Hospitals in the early 1990s by Dr William Wolberg, W. Nick Street and Olvi Mangasarian. It contains 569 patients (357 benign, 212 malignant), each with 30 measurements of the cell nuclei taken from a fine-needle aspirate (a thin needle sample of the lump).

How I worked: I worked through the project with an AI tutor (Claude) acting as my research supervisor. It explained new ideas, gave hints and helped with code. I ran the experiments, made a prediction before each one, interpreted the results and did the biology research.

Day 1: Meet the Data

Aim: explore the data before using any AI.

What I did: loaded 569 patients (357 benign, 212 malignant) with no missing data, then compared the two groups with graphs.

Result: malignant nuclei were bigger (average radius 17.5 vs 12.1) and about 3.5 times more dented, but the groups overlapped, so one measurement alone wasn't enough.

What I learned: an "AI" that says "benign" to everyone scores 62.7% but catches zero cancers, so a high score can hide serious mistakes.

Day 2: Human vs Machine

Aim: see if machine learning can beat my own rule.

What I did: made the rule "radius over 15 = malignant", then trained a Logistic Regression model on 455 patients and tested both on 114 patients the model had never seen.

Result: my rule scored 86.8%; the model scored 93.9%.

What I learned: the model wins because it combines all 30 measurements, so it catches cancers with small but irregular cells that my rule missed.

Day 3: Can We Trust It?

Aim: look at the kinds of mistakes the model makes.

What I did: sorted its guesses into four boxes and worked out the scores by hand.

Result: it caught 36 cancers but missed 6, with 1 false alarm. Accuracy was 93.9%, but sensitivity (cancers caught) was only 85.7%.

What I learned: a missed cancer is the most dangerous mistake, and high accuracy can hide it. From here on I focused on missed cancers.

Day 4: The Model Competition

Aim: compare four models on the same 114 test patients.

Model Accuracy Missed cancers
Logistic Regression 93.9% 6
Decision Tree 93.0% 4
k-Nearest Neighbours 91.2% 9
Random Forest 97.4% 3

What I learned: Random Forest (100 decision trees that vote) did best. I predicted it would miss exactly 3, which was right. I also wrote some of the model code myself.

Day 5: Making the Models Better

Aim: improve the models through experiments, predicting each result first.

Results:

  • Scaling the data cut k-NN's missed cancers from 9 to 4, because big numbers like area had been drowning out small ones like smoothness, which affected the results.
  • Letting a Decision Tree ask more questions caught more cancers but caused more false alarms (overfitting).
  • Giving Random Forest more trees helped up to 100 (trees), then made no difference.

All three of my predictions were right or mostly right.

Day 6: What Is the AI Looking At?

Aim: find which measurements the model relies on, and why.

Result: it relied most on the size and dents of the worst cells. My biology research explained why: cancer cells often have extra chromosomes, which makes the nucleus bigger, and faulty lamins (the nucleus's scaffolding), which make its outline dented.

What I learned: just because the AI relies on a feature doesn't mean that feature causes cancer. Big, dented nuclei are a sign of cancer, not its cause, like smoke and fire.

Day 7: Trying to Break the AI

Aim: find reasons a hospital should not use the model yet.

Results:

  • On 10 different random test groups, missed cancers ranged from 1 to 8, so my 97.4% was partly luck.
  • Every missed cancer was a borderline case, possibly an early cancer.
  • Measuring cells just 10% smaller, as a different microscope might, tripled the missed cancers (3 to 10).

What I learned: building a model is easier than proving it is reliable.

Day 8: Thinking Like a Real Scientist

Aim: test the models more fairly and decide how cautious they should be.

Results:

  • Lowering the caution line (the % chance needed to say "cancer") from 50% to 10% cut missed cancers from 3 to 1, but raised false alarms from 0 to 11.
  • With fairer testing (cross-validation), Logistic Regression came out on top, catching 96.2% of cancers.
  • The best setting was Logistic Regression with a 30% line: just 1 missed cancer and 1 false alarm.

What I learned: one test can crown the wrong winner, and the best setting depends on real-world costs, not just accuracy.

Day 9: Building CellScan

Aim: turn the research into an interactive app.

What I built: CellScan, where you set a patient's 30 cell measurements (or load a real patient) and see the AI's verdict. It includes a caution line, a second opinion from Random Forest, a table of real past patients, a "Why?" tab showing what pushed each decision, and a challenge mode where you try to beat the AI.

What I learned: I redesigned the measurement sliders when they gave misleading results, and fixed a freezing bug by switching to a more stable version of Gradio.

Day 10: Presenting My Findings

I wrote up the whole project and built my own website, aaryanmalik.blog, to publish it alongside my other work.

Conclusion

On this dataset, machine learning can tell malignant from benign tumours very well: the best model caught about 96% of cancers, using features that make biological sense.

But it isn't ready for real patients. It learned from only 569 patients at one hospital over 30 years ago, its results depended on luck, it struggles with borderline cancers, and a small change in equipment tripled its mistakes.

My biggest lesson: building an AI is the easy part. Proving it can be trusted is much harder.
