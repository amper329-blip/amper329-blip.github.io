Double-Entry Accounting

How every transaction has two sides, from the accounting equation and debits and credits to balance sheets, karma and incentives.

Economics· 10 min read

What is Double-Entry Accounting?

Double-entry accounting is the system used by businesses to record and track financial transactions.

A transaction is any event that causes a change in the financial position of a business.

Examples of a transaction include:

  • Selling a product
  • Paying wages
  • Buying equipment
  • Taking out a loan

Double-entry accounting is based on the principle that every transaction causes a change and affects two accounts.

The system helps businesses understand not only what has changed, but also where the money came from and where it went.

The system is used all around the world today, and has become one of the foundations of modern business, finance and investing.

Core Idea

The idea behind double-entry accounting is that every financial action has two sides. Whenever a business receives something of value, there must be a source explaining where it came from.

Likewise, whenever a business gives something up, another account must be affected.

Nothing can appear in the accounts without an explanation, and nothing can simply "disappear" from the accounts without being recorded.

This means businesses record both the effect and the cause of each transaction: for every "action" a business takes with its money, there is a matching "reaction" that keeps everything balanced.

Double-Entry Accounting and Karma

There is a similarity between double-entry accounting and karma.

In accounting, every transaction creates an effect somewhere else within the financial system.

Nothing can be gained without another account being affected.

Karma teaches that actions create consequences that eventually return to the individual.

Both concepts are based on the principle of cause and effect.

Although they are slightly different ideas, they show that actions rarely exist without consequences.

Why Double-Entry Accounting Matters

Double-entry accounting helps businesses produce reliable and accurate financial information.

The system also makes mistakes easier to identify, as transactions must balance.

It makes fraud much more difficult, as missing information creates inconsistencies.

Without accurate accounting records, businesses would struggle to understand their true financial position.

Therefore, the system plays an important role in trust within modern economies.

History of Double-Entry Accounting

Double-entry accounting became widely known during the Renaissance in Italy.

As trade expanded, merchants needed a more efficient way to track complex business activities.

The system became particularly popular among merchants in Venice.

In 1494, Luca Pacioli published a book describing the method.

Although he didn't invent the system, his work helped spread and standardise it.

Without it, managing large businesses would be far more difficult.

Transactions as Exchanges

Many people mistakenly think accounting is simply recording money.

In reality, accounting is about recording exchanges of value.

Every transaction involves something being received and something being given up.

Examples include:

  • Cash exchanged
  • Cash received from a bank loan

This way of thinking makes accounting much easier to understand, because it focuses on what actually happens.

The movement of value is often more important than the movement of money itself.

Understanding the Balance Sheet

Before learning about debits and credits, it is very useful to know what accountants are trying to keep track of.

One of the most important financial statements is called a balance sheet.

A balance sheet shows the financial position of a business at a particular point in time.

It answers three important questions:

  • What does the business own?
  • What does the business owe?
  • What belongs to the owner?

The answers to these questions are known as assets, liabilities and equity.

The Five Account Types

There are five main account types used in accounting, and each type helps organise money financially.

The five account types are:

  • Assets
  • Liabilities
  • Equity
  • Income
  • Expenses

Transactions have a huge effect on each of these account types.

Assets

Assets are things a business owns and uses to help it operate and generate future value.

Assets are important because they provide benefits that can help the business earn money.

Examples include:

  • Cash
  • Inventory
  • Equipment
  • Vehicles
  • Buildings

Most successful businesses rely on valuable assets to support growth and long-term success, creating many future opportunities for the business.

Liabilities

Liabilities are the amounts of money that a business owes to other people or organisations.

They represent debts that need to be paid back in the future.

Examples include:

  • Bank loans
  • Unpaid bills
  • Money owed to suppliers

Liabilities are not always negative; they allow and promote investment, and help the business expand.

However, excessive liabilities can create financial pressure and huge risk.

Equity

Equity represents the owner's share of the business after all liabilities are taken into consideration.

It is often described as the part of the business that truly belongs to the owners.

Equity can increase when owners invest more money into the business.

It can also increase when a business earns profits over a period of time.

Examples include:

  • Owner investments
  • Profits kept within the business

Equity is often used as a measure of long-term financial strength.

Income

Income represents value flowing into the business through its activities.

Businesses generate income by providing products, services or other forms of value.

Examples include:

  • Subscription payments
  • Service fees

Income is key because it allows businesses to grow and survive.

Without income, businesses simply run out of resources, which can lead to bankruptcy.

Expenses

Expenses are costs that businesses incur while operating.

Examples include:

  • Rent
  • Wages
  • Utilities
  • Advertising
  • Insurance

Expenses reduce profit in the short term, but in the long term, expenses such as advertising can increase profit.

Successful businesses focus on the expenses that benefit the business in the long term.

The Accounting Equation

The entire accounting system is built around a simple but impactful equation:

Assets = Liabilities + Equity
Equity = Assets − Liabilities
Liabilities = Assets − Equity

The equation shows that everything owned by a business must be funded somehow.

Businesses can either borrow money or receive funding from their owners.

Every transaction has a significant effect on the equation.

No matter what happens, the equation must stay balanced.

This equation acts as a framework that keeps the entire accounting system organised.

Why the Equation Matters

The accounting equation helps explain where business resources come from.

Imagine a business starts with:

  • Assets = £0
  • Liabilities = £0
  • Equity = £0

The business then borrows £1,000 from a bank.

Assets become £1,000, because cash has increased.

Liabilities become £1,000, because the loan must be repaid.

The equation still balances perfectly.

Understanding Debits and Credits

Debits and credits are the two sides used to record every transaction in double-entry accounting.

A debit usually records where value is going, while a credit usually records where that value came from.

Every transaction must contain at least one debit and one credit entry.

The total value of debits must always equal the total value of credits.

Debits and credits do not mean good or bad, nor do they automatically mean money in or money out.

They are simply the mechanism used to keep accounting records balanced.

Without debits and credits, double-entry accounting could not function.

Why Debits and Credits Exist

The accounting equation must remain balanced after every transaction.

Whenever something changes within a business, accountants need a way of recording the effects of the change.

Debits and credits provide the system used to record those changes.

For example, if a business buys equipment, one account increases while another account decreases.

If only one side was recorded, the accounting equation would no longer balance.

Debits and credits ensure that every transaction is fully explained.

Rules for Increases and Decreases

Different account types increase and decrease in different ways.

Account type Increases with Decreases with
Assets Debits Credits
Liabilities Credits Debits
Equity Credits Debits
Income Credits Debits
Expenses Debits Credits
  • Income increases with credits because income increases the owner's equity.
  • Expenses increase with debits because expenses reduce the owner's equity.

These rules allow accountants to record transactions consistently and keep the accounting equation balanced.

DEAD CLIC

Many students use DEAD CLIC to remember which accounts increase on each side.

DEAD accounts increase on the Debit side:

  • Drawings
  • Expenses
  • Assets

CLIC accounts increase on the Credit side:

  • Capital
  • Liabilities
  • Income

This helps simplify the debit and credit rules.

Drawings and Capital

Drawings occur when the owner removes money or resources from the business for their personal use.

Capital occurs when the owner gives money or resources to the business.

Both transactions affect the owner's share of the business.

Examples include:

  • Owner invests £1,000
  • Owner withdraws £200

Capital helps businesses grow, while drawings reduce the resources available to the business.

Understanding this difference is important when recording owner transactions.

Example: Investing Money into a Business

Imagine that an owner invests £1,000 into a business.

The business receives £1,000 in cash, which can be used to purchase assets and support operations.

At the same time, the owner has contributed value to the business, so equity increases.

Changes include:

  • Cash increases by £1,000
  • Equity increases by £1,000

This demonstrates that every financial benefit has a corresponding (equivalent) source.

Example: Taking Out a Loan

Imagine that a business borrows £500 from a bank.

The business receives cash that can be used to support growth and operations.

However, this creates a debt that must eventually be repaid.

Changes include:

  • Cash increases by £500
  • Liabilities increase by £500

This demonstrates that financial benefits often come with responsibilities.

Journal Entries

A journal entry is the first place a transaction is recorded.

Every entry shows the accounts affected, along with the debit and the credit.

Journal entries are written in date order as transactions happen.

This creates a clear record of what occurred and when.

Without journal entries, businesses would struggle to track their transactions accurately.

Ledger Accounts

Ledger accounts are where journal entries are organised by account.

Every account, such as Cash or Sales, has its own ledger.

This allows businesses to see the running total of each account over time.

The journal records transactions in order, while the ledger groups them by account.

Ledger accounts make it much easier to understand the position of each individual account.

Trial Balance

A trial balance is a check used to make sure the accounts are balanced.

It lists every account along with its debit or credit total.

The total value of debits must equal the total value of credits.

If the two sides do not match, it shows that a mistake has been made.

The trial balance is an important step before producing the final financial statements.

Balance Sheets

A balance sheet shows the financial position of a business at a particular point in time.

It displays the assets on one side, and the liabilities and equity on the other.

Both sides must always balance, following the accounting equation.

The balance sheet is created using the information from the trial balance.

This statement helps businesses, investors and owners understand the true value of the business.

Incentives and Accounting

Accounting is not only about economics, but also about human behaviour.

People often respond to rewards and punishments without even realising it (linking to a previous topic: reward and punishment).

Many people receive rewards for increasing profits or improving business performance.

This can sometimes encourage people to present financial results in a misleading way, which is very unethical.

Examples include:

  • Delaying losses
  • Exaggerating asset values
  • Manipulating financial timing

This demonstrates how incentives can influence decision-making and distort reality.
