📖 How CellScan works

CellScan is an educational demonstration. It shows how machine learning can classify breast tumour cells as benign (not cancer) or malignant (cancer). It must never be used to diagnose real patients.

🚀 Quick start

  1. Press ▶ Start exploring.
  2. In 📏 Measurements, press 🔴 Random cancer patient to load a real patient.
  3. Look at the result card at the top: CellScan's verdict and its % chance of cancer.
  4. Move the caution line (just under the result card) and see when the verdict flips.
  5. Explore the other tabs to see why it decided, how it did on real past patients, and whether you can beat it.

🟥 The result card (top of the screen)

This shows CellScan's answer for the patient currently set up in the 📏 Measurements tab. - MALIGNANT / BENIGN: CellScan's decision. - % chance of cancer: how sure the main model (Logistic Regression) is that this patient has cancer. - The coloured bar: the same % drawn as a bar. A longer bar means more likely cancer. - The black marker ▲: where your caution line is. - The sentence underneath: explains the decision, e.g. "The chance of cancer (35%) is above your 30% caution line, so CellScan says MALIGNANT."

Simple rule: if the coloured bar goes past the black marker, the verdict is malignant.

🎛️ The caution line (under the result card)

Think of it like an exam pass mark. Your score (the % chance of cancer) stays the same. The caution line is the pass mark for calling something cancer.

CellScan does two separate things for every patient: 1. Works out a % chance of cancer. The caution line does not change this. 2. Decides whether that % is high enough to call it cancer. This is what the caution line controls.

Caution line A patient with a 35% chance is called…
50% 🟢 Benign (35 is not above 50)
30% 🔴 Malignant (35 is above 30)

What it changes in the app:

Where What changes
Result card The black marker moves, and the verdict can flip
⚖️ Trade-off The missed cancers and false alarms numbers
🌲 2nd opinion Random Forest's verdict can flip
📜 Previous Patients "CellScan said", ✅/❌, and the score out of 114

📏 Measurements and 🔍 Why? Nothing

Why it starts at 30%: missing a cancer is much more dangerous than a false alarm. In testing, a 30% line gave the best balance: 1 missed cancer and 1 false alarm out of 114 test patients.

🧭 The tabs

Every tab has a ← Back button (returns to the tab you were on before, or Home) and a ↺ Reset button.

📏 Measurements

Set up a patient using 30 sliders, grouped into 4 sub-tabs: 📐 Size, 🌫️ Texture, 🕳️ Dents, 🔷 Shape. Each measurement has 3 sliders: - average cell: the typical cell in the sample - variation: how different the cells are from each other - worst cell: the most extreme cells (often the best clue for cancer)

Buttons: - 🟢 Random benign patient / 🔴 Random cancer patient: loads a real patient's 30 measurements. - ↺ Reset to average: sets every slider back to the average patient.

⚖️ Trade-off & 2nd Opinion

  • The two big numbers show what your caution line would do on 114 real test patients: how many cancers it would miss and how many false alarms it would give.
  • 🌲 Get a 2nd opinion: asks a second model (Random Forest) about the same patient. If the two models disagree, the patient is probably a borderline case. Once shown, it updates automatically.
  • ↺ Reset: caution line back to 30%, and hides the 2nd opinion.

📜 Previous Patients

A table of 114 real past patients that CellScan never trained on, with their real diagnosis, what CellScan said, and whether it was right. - Show: filter to All / Cancer only / Benign only / Mistakes only. - Choose a patient # → 📂 Load into CellScan: puts that patient's measurements into the sliders and takes you to 📏 Measurements. - ↺ Reset: back to all patients.

🔍 Why?

Shows which measurements pushed the decision for the current patient. - 🔴 Red bars push towards malignant, 🟢 green bars push towards benign. - Longer bars push harder. The numbers are the model's "points". - ↺ Reset: sets the patient back to average.

📊 The data

  • Source: the Wisconsin Diagnostic Breast Cancer dataset, UCI Machine Learning Repository.
  • Who collected it: Dr William Wolberg (surgeon), with W. Nick Street and Olvi Mangasarian, University of Wisconsin Hospitals, USA, early 1990s.
  • How: cells were taken from each lump with a thin needle (fine-needle aspirate), stained, and photographed under a microscope. A computer traced each nucleus and measured it.
  • Size: 569 patients (357 benign, 212 malignant), each diagnosis confirmed by doctors.
  • Measurements: 10 properties × 3 versions (average, variation, worst) = 30 per patient.

🤖 The models

Main model: Logistic Regression (a points system). The measurements are scaled so they're comparable. Each one earns points (positive = towards cancer). The points are added up and turned into a % chance of cancer. It learned the point values from 455 patients.

2nd opinion: Random Forest (a vote). 100 decision trees each ask yes/no questions about the measurements and vote. The % is the share voting "cancer".

🧪 How it was tested

Tested on 114 unseen patients, then with 5-fold cross-validation (5 different test groups, averaged) so results don't depend on luck.

Model Accuracy Cancers caught AUC
Logistic Regression 98.1% 96.2% 0.995
k-Nearest Neighbours 96.5% 93.4% 0.992
Random Forest 95.6% 93.0% 0.991
Decision Tree 91.7% 91.1% 0.916

🧬 The biology

  • Size: cancer cells often have extra chromosomes (aneuploidy), so the nucleus grows.
  • Dents: faulty lamins (nuclear scaffolding) make the outline lumpy.
  • Texture: DNA (chromatin) clumps unevenly, so the nucleus looks patchy.

⚠️ Important ≠ causes. These are signs of cancer, not causes.

🚫 Why this must NOT be used on real patients

  • Only 569 patients, from one hospital, over 30 years ago.
  • On different random test groups, missed cancers ranged from 1 to 8.
  • A microscope measuring cells just 10% smaller tripled missed cancers.
  • It mostly fails on borderline cancers, possibly early ones.
  • It once gave a real cancer patient only a 10% chance.
  • Never tested at another hospital.
  • Real doctors also use age, history, scans and symptoms.
